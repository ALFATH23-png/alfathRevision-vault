- Internal factors = things inside the business that it can control.

- Examples: economy, laws, competitors, customer trends, technology, environment.

You will use this distinction in:

- SWOT (Strengths/Weaknesses = internal, Opportunities/Threats = external)

4. SWOT analysis (internal + external)

What SWOT is
A tool to summarise the business situation:

- Strengths → internal positives
- Weaknesses → internal negatives
- Opportunities → external positives
- Threats → external negatives

Internal vs external here

- Strengths + Weaknesses = internal
- Opportunities + Threats = external

What examiner wants
Not just lists. They want:

- points linked to the scenario
- impact explained

How to write it
Strengths (internal)
Examples: good location, loyal customers, skilled staff, strong brand, good quality.
Write like this:

> “A key strength is the business’s strong local reputation, which increases customer loyalty and repeat sales.”

Weaknesses (internal)
Examples: limited staff, poor marketing, outdated equipment, cash flow issues.

> “A major weakness is limited online presence, which restricts access to new customers and reduces competitiveness.”

Opportunities (external)
Examples: growing demand, new trends, local events, new tech.

> “An opportunity is the growing demand for [X], which the business could use to attract new customers and increase revenue.”

Threats (external)
Examples: new competitors, economic downturn, rising costs, changing laws.

> “A threat is the increasing number of competitors in the area, which may reduce market share and put pressure on prices.”

Examiner is looking for

- Correct internal/external split
- Scenario-specific points
- Impact explained, not just named

6. Porter’s Five Forces (competition)

What it is
A tool to analyse how competitive the market is.
The 5 forces:

1. Competitive rivalry
2. Supplier power
3. Buyer power
4. Threat of new entrants
5. Threat of substitutes

All are external.
What examiner wants

- Clear explanation of each force
- Linked to the business
- Impact on profitability/strategy

How to write each one
1. Competitive rivalry

> “Competitive rivalry is high because there are many similar businesses in the area offering comparable products. This makes it harder for the business to stand out and may force it to compete on price, reducing profit margins.”

2. Supplier power

> “Supplier power is moderate/high because the business relies on a small number of suppliers for key ingredients. If suppliers increase prices, the business may have limited alternatives, which could increase costs.”

3. Buyer power

> “Buyer power is high as customers can easily switch to competitors or buy alternatives online. This means the business must maintain good value and service to retain customers.”

4. Threat of new entrants

> “The threat of new entrants is high because start-up costs are relatively low and there are few barriers to opening a similar business. This could increase competition in the future.”

5. Threat of substitutes

> “The threat of substitutes is high as customers can choose other types of food/shops or cook at home instead. This puts pressure on the business to offer something unique.”

Examiner is looking for

- Understanding of competition
- Realistic application
- Awareness of pressure on the business

7. Financial analysis (this is where you were missing definitions)

We’re going to break down every key term you might see or need to use.
7.1 Revenue
What it is:
Total money from sales.
[ \text{Revenue} = \text{Selling Price per Unit} \times \text{Quantity Sold} ]
How to write it:

> “Revenue for the period is £X, which shows the total income from sales.”
----------

7.2 Costs
Types:

- Fixed costs: stay the same regardless of output (rent, salaries).
- Variable costs: change with output (ingredients, materials).
- Total costs:
- [ \text{Total Costs} = \text{Fixed Costs} + \text{Variable Costs} ]

How to write it:

> “Fixed costs such as rent remain constant, while variable costs increase with sales. Total costs for the period are £X.”
----------

7.3 Gross profit
[ \text{Gross Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost of Sales} ]
Cost of sales = direct costs of making the product (e.g. ingredients, materials).
How to write it:

> “Gross profit is £X, showing how much the business makes after direct costs are deducted.”
----------

7.4 Net profit
[ \text{Net Profit} = \text{Gross Profit} - \text{Overheads} ]
Overheads = indirect costs (rent, admin, utilities).
How to write it:

> “Net profit is £X, which represents the final profit after all expenses are paid.”
----------

7.5 Profit margin
[ \text{Profit Margin} = \frac{\text{Profit}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100 ]
How to write it:

> “The profit margin is X%, meaning the business keeps X pence profit from every £1 of sales.”
----------

7.6 Break-even
What it is:
The number of units the business must sell to cover all costs (no profit, no loss).
[ \text{Break-even Point} = \frac{\text{Fixed Costs}}{\text{Selling Price per Unit} - \text{Variable Cost per Unit}} ]
How to write it (even if formula isn’t required):

> “The break-even point is X units, meaning the business must sell at least X units to cover its costs.”

Examiner is looking for:

- You understand it’s about minimum sales needed.
----------

7.7 Variances
This is one you said you don’t fully get. Let’s fix that.
What is a variance?
Difference between what was planned (budgeted) and what actually happened.
[ \text{Variance} = \text{Actual} - \text{Budget} ]

- Favourable variance: better than expected (e.g. higher revenue, lower costs).
- Adverse/unfavourable variance: worse than expected (e.g. lower revenue, higher costs).

How to write it:

> “There is an adverse variance of £500 in costs, meaning the business spent more than planned. This reduces profit and may indicate poor cost control.”
> “There is a favourable variance of £300 in revenue, meaning the business sold more than expected, improving profit.”

Examiner is looking for:

- You know variance = difference from budget
- You can say if it’s good or bad
- You can explain impact
----------

7.8 Cash flow
Key terms:

- Cash inflows: money coming in (sales, loans).
- Cash outflows: money going out (rent, wages, stock).
- Net cash flow: inflows − outflows.
- Opening balance: cash at start of period.
- Closing balance: cash at end of period.

[ \text{Closing Balance} = \text{Opening Balance} + \text{Net Cash Flow} ]
How to write it:

> “The business has a negative net cash flow in [month], meaning more cash is leaving than entering. This could cause cash shortages and difficulty paying bills.”

Examiner is looking for:

- You understand profit ≠ cash
- You can spot cash problems

SWOT ANALYSIS

The SWOT analysis helps a business understand its internal strengths and weaknesses, and external opportunities and threats.
It is used to support strategic decision making and identify areas for growth and risk.
It is split into internal (strengths, weaknesses) and external (opportunities, threats) factors.
Strengths (INTERNAL, POSITIVE)
Strengths are areas where the business performs well and has a competitive advantage.
These may include strong brand reputation, high product quality, skilled staff, or efficient operations.
Strengths matter because they help the business compete effectively and attract customers.
They should be maintained and built upon.
Weaknesses (INTERNAL, NEGATIVE)
Weaknesses are areas where the business is lacking or underperforming.
These may include high costs, poor marketing, limited resources, or outdated technology.
Weaknesses matter because they can reduce competitiveness and profitability.
They should be improved or minimised.
Opportunities (EXTERNAL, POSITIVE)
Opportunities are external factors the business can take advantage of to grow.
These may include market growth, new technology, changing consumer trends, or expansion opportunities.
Opportunities matter because they allow the business to increase sales and market share.
They should be exploited where possible.
Threats (EXTERNAL, NEGATIVE)
Threats are external risks that could harm the business.
These include competition, economic downturns, rising costs, and changing regulations.
Threats matter because they can reduce profits and market position.
They should be monitored and mitigated.

PESTLE ANALYSIS

PESTLE analysis helps a business analyse the external macro environment.
It identifies factors that are outside the business’s control but can impact performance and decision making.
Political
Government policies, tax changes, and regulations.
These can affect costs, operations, and profitability.
Economic
Factors such as inflation, interest rates, and economic growth.
These impact consumer spending and business costs.
Social
Consumer attitudes, lifestyles, and trends.
These influence demand and customer preferences.
Technological
Advancements in technology and innovation.
These can improve efficiency or create competitive advantage.
Legal
Laws and regulations the business must follow.
Non-compliance can result in fines or legal action.
Environmental
Environmental issues and sustainability pressures.
Businesses may need to reduce waste and improve sustainability.

----------
PORTER’S FIVE FORCES

Porter’s Five Forces helps analyse the level of competition in an industry.
It determines how attractive and profitable a market is.
Competitive Rivalry (HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW)
This refers to the level of competition between existing businesses.
High rivalry means intense competition, lower prices, and reduced profits.
Threat of New Entrants
This measures how easy it is for new businesses to enter the market.
Low barriers = more competition.
High barriers = protection for existing businesses.
Bargaining Power of Suppliers
This refers to how much control suppliers have over prices and supply.
High power means higher costs for the business.
Bargaining Power of Customers
This shows how much influence customers have.
High power means customers can demand lower prices or better quality.
Threat of Substitutes
This refers to alternative products customers can switch to.
High threat reduces demand and profitability.

----------
ANSOFF MATRIX

The Ansoff Matrix helps a business decide how to grow.
It focuses on products (new/existing) and markets (new/existing).
Market Penetration (LOW RISK)
Selling more existing products in existing markets.
This may include promotions, pricing strategies, or marketing.
It is low risk because the business already understands the market.
Market Development
Selling existing products in new markets.
This may involve expansion into new locations or customer segments.
Risk is moderate due to unfamiliar markets.
Product Development
Introducing new products to existing customers.
This keeps customers interested and increases sales.
Risk comes from product failure.
Diversification (HIGH RISK)
Selling new products in new markets.
This is the riskiest strategy due to lack of experience.
However, it can lead to high growth.

----------
BCG MATRIX

The BCG matrix helps a business decide where to invest, where to grow, and where to cut back based on a high or low: market growth and market share.
It categorises products or services into four quadrants.
Stars (HIGH MARKET SHARE, HIGH MARKET GROWTH)
STARS are products that are performing well in a fast-growing market. These products are often in high demand, they have a strong competitive position, requires investment to maintain growth. They have potential to be cash cows.
Star products are the leader of the business.
Cash cows (HIGH MARKET SHARE, LOW MARKET GROWTH)
Products that generate steady, reliable profit in a mature market. These products characteristics include: high sales, low investment needed , stable demand and high profitability.
Cash cows funds the rest of the business.
Question marks (LOW MARKET SHARE, HIGH MARKET GROWTH)
Products in a growing market but with weak performance
These products often have high potential, high risk, require investment to grow. They are called question marks because they can become stars or fail. It matters because management must decide wether to invest or discontinue the product. They require careful analysis.
Dogs (LOW MARKET SHARE, LOW MARKET GROWTH)
Products with weak performance in a slow market.
These products generate low revenue and may not be profitable.
They may be discontinued to reduce costs.

----------
PORTER’S GENERIC STRATEGIES

Porter’s Generic Strategies explain how a business can achieve competitive advantage.
Cost Leadership
The business aims to be the lowest cost producer.
This allows it to offer lower prices and attract price-sensitive customers.
Efficiency and cost control are critical.
Differentiation
The business offers unique products or services.
This allows higher pricing due to perceived value.
Branding and quality are important.
Focus Strategy
The business targets a specific niche market.
It may use cost focus or differentiation focus.
This allows the business to specialise and meet specific customer needs.

----------
USP (UNIQUE SELLING POINT)

A USP is what makes a business different from its competitors.
It is the unique feature or benefit that attracts customers.
A strong USP improves brand identity and competitive advantage.
It should be clear, specific, and valuable to customers.

----------
MARKET SHARE

Market share is a strategic performance indicator that shows how much of the total market does a business control.
Market share = percentage of total sales in a market that a business holds.
It shows how strong the business is compared to competitors, how much influence it has and how well it is performing in the market.
Market share= (business sales/total market sales) x 100
High market share – strong position
Low market share= vulnerable position.

FINANCIAL CALCULATIONS

Financial calculations are used to measure business performance and support decision making.
Revenue
Total income from sales.
Revenue = price x quantity
Costs
Fixed costs do not change (rent, salaries).
Variable costs change with output (materials).
Gross Profit
Gross profit = revenue – cost of sales
Net Profit

PERT (PROGRAM EVALUATION & REVIEW TECHNIQUE)

PERT is a planning tool used to estimate the time required for each task.
It uses three time estimates:

- Optimistic (O) → best-case scenario
- Most likely (M) → expected time
- Pessimistic (P) → worst-case scenario

The formula is:
PERT = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6
This gives a weighted average time for each task.
PERT helps improve accuracy in project planning.
It also helps identify uncertainty and risk in timelines.

The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent tasks in a project.
It determines the shortest possible time to complete the project.
Tasks on the critical path cannot be delayed.
If one task is delayed, the entire project is delayed.
Identifying the critical path helps prioritise key activities.

FLOAT / SLACK

Float (or slack) is the amount of time a task can be delayed without affecting the overall project completion.
Tasks with zero float are on the critical path.
Tasks with positive float have flexibility.
Float is important because it allows managers to:

- Reallocate resources
- Manage delays
- Improve efficiency

LEADERSHIP STYLE (DEEP EXPLANATION)

Leadership style refers to the way a manager directs, influences, and interacts with their team. It is not just about giving instructions — it shapes how decisions are made, how employees feel, and how effectively a team performs.
A key thing to understand is that leadership is not one-size-fits-all. The effectiveness of a leadership style depends heavily on the situation, the type of employees, and the nature of the task. A mismatch between leadership style and context can lead to low motivation, poor communication, and reduced productivity.
At a deeper level, leadership style influences three critical areas:

- Motivation → whether employees feel valued and driven to work
- Control → how much direction or freedom employees have
- Performance → how efficiently tasks are completed
----------

DEMOCRATIC LEADERSHIP (IN DEPTH)
Democratic leadership, also known as participative leadership, involves employees in the decision-making process. Instead of decisions being made solely by the manager, employees are encouraged to contribute ideas, opinions, and feedback.
The logic behind this style is that employees who are involved in decisions feel a greater sense of ownership and responsibility. This can lead to higher motivation and better-quality decisions, as multiple perspectives are considered.
However, this style is not purely about being “nice” or “inclusive.” It has strategic value:

- Employees closer to operations often have practical knowledge that managers may not
- Involving staff can improve problem-solving quality
- It builds a collaborative culture, which is important for long-term success

That said, democratic leadership has limitations. Because decisions require discussion and agreement, it can slow down the process. In fast-paced or high-pressure situations, this delay can reduce efficiency. Additionally, if there are conflicting opinions, it may lead to tension within the team.
So, democratic leadership works best when:

- The team is skilled and experienced
- The task benefits from creativity or input
- Time pressure is not extreme
----------

TRANSFORMATIONAL LEADERSHIP (IN DEPTH)
Transformational leadership focuses on inspiring and motivating employees to achieve more than what is expected. Instead of simply managing tasks, the leader creates a strong vision for the future and encourages employees to align with it.
This style is particularly important in situations involving change, such as introducing a new system, launching a new product, or restructuring a business. Change can often create uncertainty or resistance among employees. A transformational leader addresses this by:

- Communicating a clear and compelling vision
- Motivating employees emotionally and intellectually
- Encouraging innovation and new ideas

The deeper purpose of transformational leadership is not just to complete tasks, but to transform the mindset of employees, making them more engaged, committed, and proactive.
However, this style depends heavily on the leader’s ability to communicate and inspire. If the leader lacks credibility or clarity, the approach may fail. Additionally, transformational leadership can sometimes overlook short-term operational issues because it focuses heavily on long-term goals.
It is most effective when:

- The business is undergoing change
- Innovation or growth is required
- Employee motivation needs to be increased
----------

SITUATIONAL LEADERSHIP (IN DEPTH)
Situational leadership is based on the idea that there is no single “best” leadership style. Instead, the leader must adapt their approach depending on the situation and the needs of the team.
This requires the leader to assess:

- The skill level of employees
- Their experience
- Their confidence
- The complexity of the task

For example:

- A new employee may need clear instructions and close supervision
- An experienced employee may perform better with autonomy and trust

Situational leadership is powerful because it allows for flexibility. Instead of applying a rigid approach, the leader adjusts their behaviour to maximise performance in each specific context.
However, this style is also more demanding. It requires strong judgement, awareness, and the ability to change approach quickly. If applied poorly, it can lead to inconsistency or confusion among employees.
It is most effective when:

- Teams have mixed skill levels
- Tasks vary in complexity
- The environment is dynamic

WHY LEADERSHIP STYLE MUST FIT THE SCENARIO

The choice of leadership style is not arbitrary — it must align with the situation. This is because different scenarios create different demands.
For instance:

- A stable environment with experienced staff may benefit from democratic leadership
- A period of change requires transformational leadership
- A mixed-skill team requires situational leadership

If the wrong style is used:

- Employees may feel unsupported or micromanaged
- Decisions may be too slow or too rushed
- Motivation may decrease

Therefore, selecting the correct leadership style is a strategic decision, not just a personal preference.

MOTIVATION THEORY (DEEP EXPLANATION)

MASLOW’S HIERARCHY OF NEEDS (IN DEPTH)
Maslow proposed that human needs are structured in a hierarchy, meaning that basic needs must be satisfied before higher-level needs become important.
The five levels are:

1. Physiological needs → basic survival (e.g. salary for food and living)
2. Safety needs → job security and safe working conditions
3. Social needs → belonging, teamwork, relationships
4. Esteem needs → recognition, respect, achievement
5. Self-actualisation → personal growth and fulfilment

The key idea is that employees are not motivated by just money. While salary satisfies basic needs, higher-level motivation comes from recognition, responsibility, and achievement.
This means businesses must go beyond pay and consider:

- Workplace culture
- Opportunities for development
- Recognition systems
----------

HERZBERG’S TWO-FACTOR THEORY (IN DEPTH)
Herzberg argued that satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposites — they are influenced by different factors.
He identified:
Hygiene factors (prevent dissatisfaction)

- Pay
- Working conditions
- Job security

If these are poor, employees become dissatisfied. However, improving them does not necessarily increase motivation — it only removes dissatisfaction.

----------

Motivators (create satisfaction)

- Achievement
- Recognition
- Responsibility

These are what actually drive employees to perform better.
The key insight is:

- Businesses must first avoid dissatisfaction, then actively create motivation
----------

MCGREGOR THEORY X AND THEORY Y (IN DEPTH)
McGregor proposed two contrasting views of employees:
Theory X assumes:

- Employees dislike work
- They avoid responsibility
- They need strict supervision

This leads to authoritarian management styles.

----------

Theory Y assumes:

- Employees enjoy work
- They seek responsibility
- They are self-motivated

This leads to more empowering leadership styles.
Most modern businesses aim to operate under Theory Y because it leads to:

- Higher motivation
- Greater innovation
- Better performance

⭐ #2 — BRIEFING NOTE (FULL, ADVANCED, NO‑FAIL GUIDE)

A briefing note is a short, formal document used to quickly inform managers, stakeholders, or staff about:

- a project
- an issue
- a change
- a decision
- a problem
- a proposal

It is NOT:

- an essay
- a report
- a meeting agenda
- minutes

It is a one‑page, high‑clarity summary.

----------
⭐ 1. What a briefing note actually is

A briefing note is designed to:

- give essential information
- be read quickly
- support decision‑making
- provide clarity
- outline key points

Examiners want to see:

- clarity
- structure
- professionalism
- relevance
- conciseness
----------
⭐ 2. Where briefing notes appear in the exam

You may need a briefing note in:
✔ Task 3 — Stakeholders

- briefing stakeholders on the project
- briefing managers on concerns
- briefing the team on changes

✔ Task 4 — Leadership

- briefing staff on new processes
- briefing managers on team issues

✔ Task 5 — Coaching

- briefing a manager on an employee’s development needs

✔ Task 6 — Difficult Conversation

- briefing HR/manager on performance concerns
----------
⭐ 3. Structure of a professional briefing note

A briefing note ALWAYS includes:

1. Title
2. Purpose
3. Background
4. Key issues / key points
5. Options (if relevant)
6. Recommendation
7. Next steps

Let’s break each one down.

----------
⭐ 4. Title

Clear and specific.
Examples:

- “Briefing Note: Staff Concerns About New System”
- “Briefing Note: Stakeholder Feedback Summary”
- “Briefing Note: Performance Issue – Employee X”
----------
⭐ 5. Purpose

One sentence.

> “The purpose of this briefing note is to provide an overview of [topic] and outline key points for consideration.”
----------
⭐ 6. Background

Short, factual context.

> “The business is implementing a new ordering system to improve efficiency. Staff have raised concerns about training and workload.”
----------
⭐ 7. Key Issues / Key Points

This is the core of the briefing note.
Use bullet points.
Examples:

- Staff lack confidence using the new system
- Customers have experienced delays
- Stakeholders are concerned about communication
- Training has not been completed
- Performance has declined
----------
⭐ 8. Options (if required)

Only include if the scenario involves choosing between actions.
Examples:

- Option A: Provide additional training
- Option B: Adjust staffing levels
- Option C: Delay implementation
----------
⭐ 9. Recommendation

Clear, justified.

> “It is recommended that additional training is provided before full implementation, as this will reduce errors and improve confidence.”
----------
⭐ 10. Next Steps

Short, actionable.

> “Training sessions will be scheduled for next week. A follow‑up review will take place on 10/05/2026.”
----------

Support available
